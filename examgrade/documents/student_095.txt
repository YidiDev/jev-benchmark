Q (q02, 4 pts): Explain the significance of the Indian Ocean trade network before 1500 CE.
A: The Indian Ocean trade network connected regions like East Africa's Swahili coast, Arabia, India, Southeast Asia, and China into one interconnected system. This was possible because of the monsoon winds, which reversed direction predictably with the seasons, so sailors could plan round trips knowing when the winds would blow one way and then the other. Along with trade came the spread of Islam, which merchants and travelers carried to the East African coast and into Southeast Asia, changing the religious landscape of those regions. This network was one of the most significant premodern systems connecting distant cultures.

Q (q09, 4 pts): Describe the Columbian Exchange and its effects.
A: The Columbian Exchange refers to the widespread transfer of crops, livestock, people, and diseases between the Americas and Afro-Eurasia beginning after 1492. Old World diseases like smallpox devastated indigenous American populations, since they had no prior immunity and had never been exposed to these pathogens before. On the other hand, New World crops such as maize and potatoes were brought to Afro-Eurasia, where they boosted food supplies and contributed to significant population growth. At the same time, European livestock and crops introduced to the Americas transformed local ecosystems and diets, fundamentally reshaping societies on both sides of the Atlantic.

Q (q10, 3 pts): Explain the significance of the Tokugawa Shogunate's sakoku policy.
A: The sakoku policy severely restricted foreign trade and travel, essentially closing Japan off from most contact with the outside world. The Tokugawa Shogunate implemented this mainly to curb the spread of Christianity, which they saw as destabilizing, and to limit the political and economic influence that foreign powers might gain within Japan. This isolationist stance shaped Japan's development for over two centuries.

Q (q15, 4 pts): Explain the process of industrialization in Britain.
A: Industrialization in Britain involved a shift from home-based cottage industry to centralized factories, changing where and how goods were produced. This was made possible in part by Britain's abundant domestic supplies of coal and iron, which fueled machinery and factory production. As factories grew, workers moved from rural areas into industrial cities in a rapid wave of urbanization, and conditions in these growing cities were often harsh and overcrowded.

Q (q16, 4 pts): Explain the causes of European imperialism in Africa in the late 19th century.
A: European imperialism in Africa was driven by industrial economies' desire for raw materials and new markets to sell manufactured goods. European powers also competed intensely with one another for territory and prestige, a rivalry that was formalized through agreements like the Berlin Conference, which divided Africa among European claims. This expansion was justified using Social Darwinist and racial ideologies that framed domination as a supposed "civilizing mission" for African peoples. Finally, superior military technology, such as the Maxim gun, gave European powers a decisive advantage that allowed them to conquer and control African territories with relatively small forces.

Q (q19, 4 pts): Explain the causes of World War I.
A: World War I was caused by a complex web of alliances among European powers, which meant that a conflict between just two countries could quickly pull in the rest of the continent. Underlying this was ongoing tension from competing nationalist and imperial ambitions among the great powers, who each sought greater influence and territory. The immediate spark for the war was the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand in Sarajevo in 1914, which set off the chain of alliance obligations. Additionally, a prewar arms race, including intense naval competition between Britain and Germany, had already heightened tensions and readiness for war before the assassination even occurred.
