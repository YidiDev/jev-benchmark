Q (q01, 3 pts): Describe the political and administrative structure the Mongol Empire used to govern its territory.
A: The Mongol Empire was founded by Genghis Khan, who unified the Mongol tribes and built a massive empire stretching across Asia. One thing that made this work was that it allowed the Mongols to quickly mobilize and command a huge population made up of many different ethnic groups, without having to depend on the old tribal and clan hierarchies that existed before. This meant loyalty was tied more to the empire and its leadership than to old kinship ties, which helped them rule so much territory.

Q (q02, 4 pts): Explain the significance of the Indian Ocean trade network before 1500 CE.
A: Indian Ocean trade moved spices, textiles, porcelain, gold. Big stuff. Monsoon winds switched direction seasonally. Sailors used that to time trips there and back. Also Islam spread along the way. Went to East African coast. Went to Southeast Asia too. Merchants and travelers carried it along the routes.

Q (q03, 3 pts): Describe the circumstances of the fall of the Byzantine Empire in 1453.
A: So the fall of Byzantium in 1453 really comes down to the Ottomans, specifically Mehmed II, finally taking Constantinople after the city had survived so many sieges before. What made this one different, and I keep coming back to this, is the massive cannons the Ottomans used, these huge siege guns that were able to actually break through the walls that had protected the city for like a thousand years, walls that had held off basically everyone before. Once those walls came down the city fell, and that was it, that was the end of the Roman Empire's imperial line, since Byzantium had basically been continuing Rome this whole time. And beyond just ending that political line, it also meant the Ottomans now controlled the key trade routes in the Eastern Mediterranean connecting Europe to Asia, which is actually a big deal because it pushed European powers to start looking for alternate sea routes to Asia, which as we know leads into the Age of Exploration. So it's kind of this one event, the siege and the cannons breaking the walls, that ripples out into ending an empire and reshaping global trade patterns.

Q (q04, 3 pts): Explain the impact of the Black Death on European society and economy.
A: The Black Death caused a major labor shortage across Europe because so many workers died. This drove wages up for the surviving peasants and weakened the manorial system, since lords needed to offer better terms to keep workers, which helped speed along the decline of feudalism. It also caused a religious crisis, since people prayed and the plague kept spreading anyway, leading some to question the Church's authority, and minority groups like Jews were often scapegoated and blamed for the disease.

Q (q05, 2 pts): Describe how the Aztec Empire extracted resources from the peoples it conquered.
A: Aztecs used tribute. Conquered city-states had to pay. Goods, labor, soldiers. Regular payments. That's the system.

Q (q06, 3 pts): Explain how the Ottoman Empire used the devshirme system.
A: I think the devshirme system worked by the Ottomans periodically taking Christian boys, mostly from the Balkan provinces, as a kind of levy or tax on the population there. From what I remember, these boys were then converted to Islam and put through training, and a lot of them ended up becoming elite soldiers known as Janissaries, or in some cases they rose up to become high officials in the administration. I'm not totally sure but I think the point of this whole system was that it gave the Sultan a military and administrative class that was loyal directly to him personally, rather than to any hereditary noble families, which would have been a threat to his power. So it kind of let the Ottomans build a dependable elite that owed everything to the state itself.

Q (q07, 5 pts): Explain the significance of the Atlantic Slave Trade for the development of the Americas.
A: Okay so the Atlantic Slave Trade, this is basically the triangular trade system, where you've got Europe, West Africa, and the Americas all connected, and millions of enslaved Africans were forcibly shipped across the Atlantic as part of this. And once they arrived in the Americas they were put to forced labor on plantations, growing cash crops like sugar, tobacco, cotton, these labor-intensive crops that needed huge workforces. And this whole system generated enormous wealth, like really enormous, for the European trading empires and their colonial economies, this is part of what fueled European economic growth for centuries. But going back to Africa, the flip side of all this wealth being generated elsewhere is that West and Central Africa were depopulated and destabilized over the centuries this went on, losing so many people and having political structures disrupted by the slave raiding. And then there's also the lasting cultural impact, because enslaved Africans brought their languages, religions, music, and traditions with them, and that influence is still visible across the Americas today, so it's not just an economic story, it's also a lasting cultural legacy.

Q (q08, 3 pts): Explain the causes of the Protestant Reformation.
A: The Protestant Reformation began with Martin Luther posting his Ninety-Five Theses in 1517, which criticized the Catholic Church. A major target of his criticism was the sale of indulgences and the broader sense that the Church had become corrupt. His ideas were able to spread quickly because of the printing press, which allowed his writings to circulate widely across Europe, and some German princes supported the Reformation for their own political and economic reasons, such as wanting to weaken the power of the Church and the Holy Roman Emperor.

Q (q09, 4 pts): Describe the Columbian Exchange and its effects.
A: Old World diseases like smallpox spread to the Americas and devastated indigenous populations since they had no immunity to these illnesses. On the other side, New World crops like maize and potatoes were brought to Afro-Eurasia and boosted food supplies there, which helped population growth. European livestock and crops also transformed the ecosystems and diets of the Americas, so both sides of the Atlantic experienced major changes to how societies lived and ate.

Q (q10, 3 pts): Explain the significance of the Tokugawa Shogunate's sakoku policy.
A: Sakoku closed Japan off. Almost no foreign trade or travel allowed. Shogunate wanted to stop Christianity spreading. Also wanted to limit foreign influence, political and economic. Still let Dutch trade a bit. Through Nagasaki. At Dejima island. That was the one exception.

Q (q11, 3 pts): Explain the causes of the Scientific Revolution in Europe.
A: One major cause of the Scientific Revolution was a new emphasis on empirical observation and experimentation as the basis for gaining knowledge, rather than just accepting things on faith. This directly challenged the older reliance on classical texts, like the works of Aristotle, and on religious doctrine as the only sources of truth about the natural world. This shift in thinking pushed scholars to test ideas for themselves instead of just trusting ancient authorities.

Q (q12, 4 pts): Explain how Enlightenment political philosophy influenced later revolutions.
A: Enlightenment thinkers introduced ideas like natural rights, the social contract, and separation of powers, which pushed back against absolute monarchy. Figures like Locke, Rousseau, and Montesquieu were key in developing these political theories. These same ideas directly fed into the French Revolution, where people called for liberty, equality, and an end to absolute royal power, showing how philosophy translated into real political action.

Q (q13, 5 pts): Describe the causes and effects of the French Revolution.
A: Thinking through the French Revolution, it really starts with the financial mess the French government was in, like chronic debt made way worse by years of expensive wars, including helping fund the American Revolution, and the monarchy just couldn't pay for everything anymore. On top of that there was so much resentment building up over the rigid estates system, where the nobility and clergy had all these privileges and barely paid taxes while the common people carried the burden, so that inequality was a huge driver too. And then Enlightenment ideas were circulating, ideas about liberty, equality, popular sovereignty, giving people a language and a framework to actually challenge the monarchy instead of just accepting it. All of this combined led to the monarchy being overthrown, and eventually Louis XVI himself was executed, which was just a massive, shocking break from the old order. But then after all that revolutionary chaos and instability, things didn't just settle into a stable republic, instead the instability actually opened the door for Napoleon Bonaparte to rise to power, so in a way the revolution that was supposed to end tyranny ended up paving the way for a new kind of strongman rule.

Q (q14, 4 pts): Explain the significance of the Haitian Revolution.
A: The Haitian Revolution was significant because it was the only successful large-scale slave revolt that actually led to the creation of an independent nation, Haiti. As a result of the revolution, France lost what had been its most profitable colony, which weakened its overall position in the Americas. The revolution also inspired enslaved and colonized peoples in other parts of the world who saw that liberation was possible, while at the same time it alarmed other slaveholding powers who feared similar uprisings in their own territories.

Q (q15, 4 pts): Explain the process of industrialization in Britain.
A: Industrialization in Britain involved a shift away from home-based cottage industry, where goods were made in individual households, toward centralized factories where production was consolidated. This was helped along by Britain having abundant domestic coal and iron deposits, which provided the raw materials and energy needed for industrial production. As factories grew, there was rapid urbanization, with workers moving into industrial cities to find jobs, often living and working under pretty harsh and crowded conditions.

And ideology mattered. Social Darwinism used. Racial ideas too. Called it a civilizing mission to justify control.

Q (q18, 3 pts): Explain the significance of the Meiji Restoration in Japan.
A: So with the Meiji Restoration, the big thing is that the Tokugawa Shogunate, which had ruled Japan for centuries, was ended, and imperial rule was formally brought back, at least in name, with the emperor restored as the symbolic head. But really the significance goes beyond just that political change, because Japan used this moment to rapidly modernize, like really fast, modernizing its military, its industry, its whole government structure, basically copying Western models to catch up technologically and institutionally. And because of all that rapid modernization, Japan managed to become a major industrial and military power in its own right, which is huge because it meant Japan avoided being colonized like so much of the rest of Asia was, and instead it turned around and started projecting its own imperial power onto its neighbors later on. So it's this whole cycle of ending old feudal rule, modernizing fast, and then becoming a colonizer itself instead of being colonized.

Q (q19, 4 pts): Explain the causes of World War I.
A: One cause of WWI was the complicated web of alliances across Europe, which meant that a conflict between just two powers could quickly pull in all the other major nations. The immediate spark was the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand in Sarajevo in 1914, which set the crisis into motion. There was also a prewar arms race happening, including naval competition between Britain and Germany, which made all the major powers more prepared and willing to go to war than they might have been otherwise.

Q (q29, 3 pts): Describe the effects of globalization on the world economy since 1990.
A: Thinking about globalization since 1990, one of the main things is that trade barriers came down a lot, like tariffs and restrictions got reduced, and this let supply chains become way more interconnected across different countries, so a product might have parts made in like five different places before it's finished. And along with that, advances in digital communication, like the internet basically, really sped up how fast capital, information, and business could move across borders, so things that used to take a long time now happen almost instantly. But it's not all positive connection and flow, because this increased cultural exchange, people being exposed to so many other cultures and influences, has also caused a backlash in a lot of places, with movements popping up that want to defend local or national identity against what they see as outside influence, so there's this tension between globalizing forces and people pushing back to protect their own traditions.
