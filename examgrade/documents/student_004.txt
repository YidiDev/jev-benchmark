Q (q01, 3 pts): Describe the political and administrative structure the Mongol Empire used to govern its territory.
A: I think the Mongols organized their people using some kind of decimal system, like breaking everyone into groups of 10, 100, 1,000, and 10,000? Q (q07, 5 pts): Explain the significance of the Atlantic Slave Trade for the development of the Americas.

Q (q10, 3 pts): Explain the significance of the Tokugawa Shogunate's sakoku policy.
A: I think the sakoku policy was really significant because it pretty much shut Japan off from most of the outside world, severely restricting foreign trade and travel for a long stretch of time. I believe the Shogunate did this partly because they wanted to stop Christianity from spreading further in Japan, and also to limit how much foreign powers could influence Japan's politics and economy. But I'm fairly sure it wasn't a complete and total shutdown, because the Dutch were still allowed some limited trade, through the controlled port of Nagasaki, at that man-made island called Dejima I believe, so Japan kept just a small window open to the outside world even while isolating itself otherwise.

Q (q11, 3 pts): Explain the causes of the Scientific Revolution in Europe.
A: Okay so the Scientific Revolution, I think what really drove it was this new emphasis on actually observing things and doing experiments to figure out how the world worked, instead of just trusting old assumptions. And that's kind of the key point I keep circling back to, this empirical approach really challenged the old reliance on classical texts, like stuff from Aristotle, and it also challenged just accepting religious doctrine as the only source of truth about the natural world. So people started saying, well, let's test this instead of just believing it because some ancient authority said so. And you see this playing out with figures like Copernicus, who proposed heliocentrism, that the sun rather than earth was the center, and then later Galileo built on that and got in trouble with the church for it, and eventually Newton came along with his own big discoveries about motion and gravity. So overall it's this combination of new methods of inquiry plus specific people making groundbreaking discoveries that pushed European thought in a whole new direction.

Q (q12, 4 pts): Explain how Enlightenment political philosophy influenced later revolutions.
A: I think Enlightenment philosophy really centered around ideas like natural rights, the social contract, and separation of powers, which were all about limiting the power of any one ruler and protecting people's freedoms. I believe thinkers like Locke, Rousseau, and Montesquieu were central to developing these ideas, though I'm not totally sure which one came up with which specific concept exactly. I do think these ideas fed directly into the French Revolution, since revolutionaries were calling for liberty, equality, and wanted to end the absolute monarchy that existed under the king, so there's a pretty direct link between the philosophy and what actually happened in France.

Q (q14, 4 pts): Explain the significance of the Haitian Revolution.
A: So the Haitian Revolution, I think what stands out most is that Toussaint Louverture led the enslaved population there in this huge uprising against their colonial masters, and what's crazy is that it actually worked, like it became the only successful large-scale slave revolt in history that led to founding an independent nation, which became Haiti. And I keep thinking about how big a deal that must have been for France too, because losing Haiti meant France lost its most profitable colony, like the sugar and other exports from there were huge money makers, so that really weakened France's whole position in the Americas going forward. And then beyond just France, I think the revolution had this ripple effect where it inspired other enslaved and colonized people elsewhere to think that maybe they could rise up too, while at the same time it terrified slaveholding powers everywhere, like the US and other colonial powers, because it showed them that a slave revolt really could succeed and overthrow the existing order.

Q (q16, 4 pts): Explain the causes of European imperialism in Africa in the late 19th century.
A: So when I think about why European powers moved into Africa in the late 1800s, I keep coming back to a few overlapping reasons. First there's the economic angle, like these industrial economies in Europe needed raw materials and also new markets to sell their goods, so Africa looked like a solution to both problems. Then there's also just competition between the European powers themselves, like a prestige thing, where nobody wanted to be left behind while rivals grabbed territory, and this rivalry got formalized at things like the Berlin Conference where they basically divided up Africa amongst themselves. And then there's the ideological piece too, where a lot of Europeans used Social Darwinist and racial ideas to convince themselves this was some kind of "civilizing mission," like they were doing Africa a favor by dominating it, which is obviously a really twisted justification looking back. And finally, technology mattered a ton, because with things like the Maxim gun, Europeans had this huge military advantage that let them overpower African resistance pretty decisively even when outnumbered.
