Q (q01, 3 pts): Describe the political and administrative structure the Mongol Empire used to govern its territory.
A: Okay so the Mongol Empire, which was founded by Genghis Khan, used this really interesting system to govern all the land they conquered, and it basically came down to organizing people by a decimal system, so like units of 10, then 100, then 1,000, then 10,000. I keep coming back to this because it's kind of the key to the whole thing - by breaking people into these numerical units instead of keeping them in their old tribal or clan groups, Genghis Khan basically dissolved the old loyalties that people had to their specific tribes. So instead of owing allegiance to your clan chief, you owed allegiance to the leader of your unit of 10 or 1,000, which ultimately traced back up to the Khan himself. And what this let them do, and I think this is really the significance of it, is mobilize this massive multi-ethnic population really fast for military campaigns without having to deal with a ton of competing tribal hierarchies getting in the way. So basically the decimal system was both a military tool and an administrative tool at the same time, cutting across old loyalties to create something more unified and controllable across this huge empire.

Q (q02, 4 pts): Explain the significance of the Indian Ocean trade network before 1500 CE.
A: The Indian Ocean trade network before 1500 was honestly huge for connecting a ton of different regions, and I keep thinking about how many places it touched - like East Africa along the Swahili coast, Arabia, India, Southeast Asia, and China were all linked together through this system. The goods that moved through this network included things like spices, textiles, porcelain, and gold, so you had this constant flow of valuable stuff moving between all these different regions. What made this whole trade system work so well, and I think this is a really important point, was the monsoon winds - they would reverse direction predictably with the seasons, so sailors could actually plan round trip voyages knowing the winds would blow one way for part of the year and then switch back the other way later. This predictability is what really let this maritime trade network flourish the way it did. And then on top of the actual goods being traded, there was also this cultural and religious spread happening, especially the spread of Islam, which traveled along the East African coast and into Southeast Asia through merchants and travelers who were going back and forth as part of this trade. So overall the network was significant for both economic exchange and this religious/cultural diffusion of Islam.

Q (q03, 3 pts): Describe the circumstances of the fall of the Byzantine Empire in 1453.
A: So in 1453 the Byzantine Empire finally fell when the Ottoman Empire, led by Mehmed II, managed to capture the city of Constantinople after it had survived so many sieges before that. I keep thinking about how the walls of Constantinople were basically legendary for being impossible to break through, but the Ottomans were able to get through them this time because they had these massive siege cannons that could actually blast through the ancient walls, which is a big part of why the siege finally succeeded where others had failed. That's really the main thing that happened - Mehmed II's forces using this new cannon technology to bring down defenses that had held for centuries.

Q (q04, 3 pts): Explain the impact of the Black Death on European society and economy.
A: I think the Black Death had a pretty massive impact on Europe, killing something like a third of the population in the mid-1300s, which is a huge number when you think about it. I'm not totally sure of the exact statistics but that's roughly what I remember. Because so many people died, there was suddenly a labor shortage, and I believe this actually gave surviving peasants and workers more bargaining power, so wages went up and this ended up weakening the whole manorial system and serfdom, kind of speeding along the decline of feudalism that was already happening in some ways. I also think, though I'm not 100% sure how directly connected this is, that the plague caused a lot of religious turmoil - people were scapegoating minority groups like Jewish communities, blaming them for the disease, and there was also this broader crisis of faith because people prayed so much for the plague to stop and it just kept killing people anyway, which made some people start questioning the Church's authority and its ability to explain or stop the suffering. So overall I think the Black Death reshaped European society in demographic, economic, and religious ways all at once.

Q (q05, 2 pts): Describe how the Aztec Empire extracted resources from the peoples it conquered.
A: The Aztec Empire mainly extracted resources through a tribute system. Conquered city-states were required to regularly send goods, labor, or soldiers to the Aztec rulers as a form of tribute. This system was important because it allowed the empire to fund its capital city, Tenochtitlan, and support its elite class without having to directly administer or govern every single conquered territory itself. So instead of installing Aztec officials everywhere, they let local rulers stay in charge as long as tribute kept flowing in.

Q (q06, 3 pts): Explain how the Ottoman Empire used the devshirme system.
A: The devshirme system took people who were converted to Islam and then trained for service. Many became Janissary soldiers. Others became high administrators. This gave the Sultan a loyal military and administrative class. They were tied to him personally. Not to hereditary nobility. It helped centralize power under the Sultan.

Q (q07, 5 pts): Explain the significance of the Atlantic Slave Trade for the development of the Americas.
A: The Atlantic Slave Trade connected Europe, West Africa, and the Americas. This was the triangular trade. Millions of Africans were forcibly transported across the ocean. They were forced into labor on plantations. These plantations grew cash crops. Sugar, tobacco, cotton. This labor generated huge wealth for European empires and colonial economies. Back in Africa, the trade caused major depopulation. It destabilized parts of West and Central Africa for centuries. In the Americas, African culture left a lasting mark. Religion, language, music, all influenced by African traditions that survived and spread.

Q (q08, 3 pts): Explain the causes of the Protestant Reformation.
A: So the Protestant Reformation really kicks off with Martin Luther and his Ninety-Five Theses in 1517, where he's basically nailing up this list of complaints against the Catholic Church, and I keep coming back to the fact that a huge part of his complaint was about the sale of indulgences - basically people paying money to reduce punishment for sins, which Luther saw as corrupt and against what he thought Christianity should be about. So there's this whole issue of corruption within the Church that's bothering him and a lot of other people too. And then once he puts these ideas out there, they spread incredibly fast, way faster than reform ideas could have spread before, because of the printing press - suddenly you can mass produce these pamphlets and get Luther's arguments out to tons of people quickly. And it wasn't just religious enthusiasm driving this either, some German princes actually backed Luther and the reform movement for their own political and economic reasons, like wanting more independence from the Catholic Church and the Holy Roman Emperor. So it's really this combination of religious grievance, corruption, and the printing press plus political self-interest that caused the Reformation to take off.

Q (q09, 4 pts): Describe the Columbian Exchange and its effects.
A: The Columbian Exchange refers to the transfer of crops, livestock, people, and diseases between the Americas and Afro-Eurasia after Columbus's voyages in 1492. One major effect was the devastating impact of Old World diseases like smallpox on indigenous populations in the Americas, who had no prior immunity and died in massive numbers as a result. On the other side, New World crops such as maize and potatoes were brought to Afro-Eurasia, where they boosted food supplies and contributed to population growth across Europe, Africa, and Asia. Meanwhile, European livestock and crops introduced to the Americas transformed local ecosystems and diets there. Altogether these exchanges reshaped societies and environments on both sides of the Atlantic.

Q (q10, 3 pts): Explain the significance of the Tokugawa Shogunate's sakoku policy.
A: The sakoku policy was mainly meant to curb the spread of Christianity in Japan and limit how much foreign powers could influence Japanese politics and economics. The shogunate was worried about European missionaries and traders gaining too much sway. That said, the policy wasn't a complete shutdown of all contact, since limited Dutch trade was still allowed through the controlled port of Nagasaki at Dejima, giving Japan a small but carefully managed window to the outside world.

Q (q12, 4 pts): Explain how Enlightenment political philosophy influenced later revolutions.
A: Enlightenment thinkers came up with a bunch of important political ideas, like natural rights, the social contract, and separation of powers, and I keep going back to how these ideas were kind of revolutionary because they challenged the old idea that monarchs just had divine authority to rule however they wanted. Thinkers like Locke, Rousseau, and Montesquieu were central to developing these concepts - Locke with natural rights and the social contract, Montesquieu with separation of powers, stuff like that. And these ideas didn't just stay in philosophy books, they directly fed into the French Revolution, where people were calling for liberty, equality, and an end to absolute monarchy, basically taking the Enlightenment ideals and turning them into a political movement demanding real change. So there's this direct line from the philosophical ideas to actual revolutionary action against the monarchy in France.

Q (q13, 5 pts): Describe the causes and effects of the French Revolution.
A: I think the French Revolution had a bunch of causes that all built on each other. One big one, I believe, was the chronic debt the French government was in, made worse by expensive wars like the American Revolution that France helped fund. There was also, I think, a lot of resentment toward the rigid estates system, where the nobility and clergy had all these privileges and didn't really pay taxes while the common people were stuck carrying the burden, which understandably made people pretty angry. On top of that, Enlightenment ideas about liberty, equality, and popular sovereignty were circulating and giving people a language and framework to actually challenge the monarchy's legitimacy. As for the effects, I'm fairly confident that the monarchy was overthrown and Louis XVI was eventually executed, which was a pretty dramatic outcome. And then afterward, I think the instability and chaos that followed, with different factions fighting for control, eventually created the conditions that let Napoleon Bonaparte rise to power and take over. So overall it's this mix of financial crisis, social inequality, and new political ideas leading to revolution and eventual authoritarian takeover.

Q (q14, 4 pts): Explain the significance of the Haitian Revolution.
A: The Haitian Revolution is such a significant event, and I keep thinking about how it was led by Toussaint Louverture, who helped organize the enslaved population into an uprising against French colonial rule. What makes it stand out so much, and I think this is the core of its significance, is that it was the only successful large-scale slave revolt in history that actually led to the founding of a fully independent nation, which became Haiti. Because of this revolution, France lost what had been its most profitable colony, which really weakened France's overall position and wealth in the Americas. And beyond just the immediate outcome, the revolution had this huge ripple effect - it inspired enslaved and colonized people in other parts of the world who saw that a slave revolt could actually succeed, while at the same time it deeply alarmed other slaveholding powers who worried the same thing could happen in their own colonies. So it's significant both for what it directly achieved and for the fear and inspiration it spread elsewhere.

Q (q16, 4 pts): Explain the causes of European imperialism in Africa in the late 19th century.
A: There's a lot going on with why European powers pushed into Africa in the late 1800s, and I keep circling back to the economic angle first - basically industrial economies in Europe needed raw materials and new markets to keep growing, and Africa had a lot of resources they wanted. But it wasn't just economics, there was also this intense competition between European powers themselves, like a prestige rivalry where nobody wanted to be left behind, and this got formalized at things like the Berlin Conference where they basically carved up Africa among themselves. Then there's the ideological side too, which I think is really important - Europeans used Social Darwinist ideas and racial theories to justify what they were doing, framing their conquest as some kind of "civilizing mission" instead of just raw exploitation. And finally none of this would have been possible without the military technology gap, especially things like the Maxim gun, which gave European forces a massive advantage over African societies that didn't have equivalent weapons. So it's really this combination of economic need, political rivalry, racist ideology, and technological superiority all feeding into imperialism in Africa.
