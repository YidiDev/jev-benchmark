Q (q01, 3 pts): Describe the political and administrative structure the Mongol Empire used to govern its territory.
A: Genghis Khan founded the Mongol Empire. It was huge. Many different ethnic groups lived under it. The system let Mongols quickly mobilize armies and control lots of people. Didn't need old tribal or clan chains of command. Just moved fast and ruled directly through loyalty to the khan.

Q (q02, 4 pts): Explain the significance of the Indian Ocean trade network before 1500 CE.
A: Indian Ocean trade moved spices. Also textiles. Porcelain too. Gold moved along it. Islam spread along the East African coast. Also spread into Southeast Asia. Merchants and travelers carried it. That's the main stuff.

Q (q03, 3 pts): Describe the circumstances of the fall of the Byzantine Empire in 1453.
A: Ottomans used massive cannons. Broke through the old walls. City finally fell after long siege. This ended the Roman imperial line for good. Also gave Ottomans control of eastern Mediterranean trade routes. That pushed Europeans to look for new routes to Asia.

Q (q04, 3 pts): Explain the impact of the Black Death on European society and economy.
A: Black Death killed about a third of Europe's population. Happened mid 1300s. Huge labor shortage after. Wages went up. Serfdom and manorial system got weaker. Feudalism started declining faster because of this.

Q (q05, 2 pts): Describe how the Aztec Empire extracted resources from the peoples it conquered.
A: So the Aztecs, they conquered a lot of peoples around them, and I keep thinking about how they didn't really run every single territory themselves, like they weren't out there micromanaging every conquered city or town. Instead it seems like the whole point was to get resources flowing back to Tenochtitlan, the capital, and to support the elite class there. So basically the empire's wealth and power was centered on funding that capital and the nobles and priests and rulers at the top, without them having to directly administer every place they took over. I guess that means it was kind of an indirect system, but I'm blanking on exactly how they made that work day to day, so I'll just say it funded Tenochtitlan and the elite without needing full control over every conquered area.

Q (q06, 3 pts): Explain how the Ottoman Empire used the devshirme system.
A: I think the devshirme system was basically a periodic levy where the Ottomans took Christian boys from the Balkan provinces, sort of like a tax in people rather than money. From there, I believe these boys were converted to Islam and given training, and a lot of them ended up becoming elite Janissary soldiers, while others rose to become high-ranking administrators in the empire. I'm fairly confident the whole point of this was to build a military and administrative class that was loyal directly to the Sultan personally, rather than to some hereditary noble families who might have their own agendas. So it kind of let the Sultan have a power base that owed everything to him instead of to birthright or local elites.

Q (q07, 5 pts): Explain the significance of the Atlantic Slave Trade for the development of the Americas.
A: I think the Atlantic Slave Trade worked as a triangular system connecting Europe, West Africa, and the Americas, and it forcibly moved millions of enslaved Africans across the ocean. I'm pretty sure a lot of these people ended up doing forced labor on plantations, growing things like sugar, tobacco, and cotton. I also believe this trade caused a lot of depopulation and instability in parts of West and Central Africa over a long stretch of time, since so many people were taken away. I'm not totally sure about all the deeper effects but those seem like the main points.

Q (q08, 3 pts): Explain the causes of the Protestant Reformation.
A: I think the Protestant Reformation really got going when Martin Luther posted his Ninety-Five Theses in 1517, criticizing the Catholic Church. From what I remember, he was especially upset about the sale of indulgences and what he saw as corruption within the Church more broadly. I'm also fairly confident that the printing press played a big role in letting his ideas spread really quickly across Europe, faster than earlier reform attempts could have spread. And I believe some German princes ended up supporting the reform movement too, partly for political and economic reasons, like wanting more independence from the Church's authority and wanting to keep church wealth for themselves.

Q (q09, 4 pts): Describe the Columbian Exchange and its effects.
A: Columbian Exchange happened after 1492. Crops, livestock, people, diseases moved between Americas and Afro-Eurasia. Smallpox and other Old World diseases wrecked native populations. They had no immunity. European livestock and crops changed American ecosystems. Changed diets too. Reshaped societies on both sides of the ocean.

Q (q10, 3 pts): Explain the significance of the Tokugawa Shogunate's sakoku policy.
A: Sakoku severely restricted foreign trade and travel. Japan closed itself off from most of the outside world. The Shogunate wanted to stop Christianity from spreading. They also wanted to limit foreign political and economic influence over Japan. Those were the main goals behind the policy.

Q (q11, 3 pts): Explain the causes of the Scientific Revolution in Europe.
A: Okay so thinking about the Scientific Revolution, I think the big thing was this new emphasis on actually observing the world and doing experiments, rather than just trusting old authorities. Like people started to believe you should test things empirically to get real knowledge. And that kind of directly challenged the old reliance on classical texts, especially Aristotle, and also religious doctrine, as the only sources of truth people could turn to. So instead of just accepting what the ancients said, thinkers started checking things themselves. Figures like Copernicus, Galileo, and Newton come to mind here, with stuff like heliocentrism, the idea that the earth revolves around the sun instead of the other way around, which went against church teaching at the time. So basically it's this combination of new methods plus specific discoveries that pushed European thought in a new direction, away from just accepting ancient or religious authority.

Q (q12, 4 pts): Explain how Enlightenment political philosophy influenced later revolutions.
A: Enlightenment thinkers developed ideas about natural rights, meaning rights people have simply by being human. They also talked about the social contract, the idea that government's power comes from the consent of the governed. Separation of powers was another key concept, dividing government into different branches to prevent abuse. These ideas directly shaped the American Revolution, since colonists used arguments about natural rights and self-government to justify breaking away from Britain.

Q (q13, 5 pts): Describe the causes and effects of the French Revolution.
A: Government was deep in debt. Costly wars made it worse. People resented the estates system. Nobles and clergy had special privileges. Commoners were angry about that. Enlightenment ideas spread. Liberty, equality, popular sovereignty. These pushed revolution forward. Instability followed the revolution. Napoleon eventually rose to power out of that chaos.

Q (q14, 4 pts): Explain the significance of the Haitian Revolution.
A: I think the Haitian Revolution was led by Toussaint Louverture, who guided the enslaved population's uprising against French colonial rule. From what I remember it's significant because it was the only successful large-scale slave revolt in history that actually led to founding an independent nation, which became Haiti. I believe France lost what had been its most profitable colony because of this, which weakened its overall position in the Americas. I'm also fairly sure the revolution inspired enslaved and colonized people elsewhere who saw that freedom was possible, while at the same time it alarmed other slaveholding powers who worried similar revolts could happen in their own territories.

Q (q15, 4 pts): Explain the process of industrialization in Britain.
A: I think one of the big things driving industrialization in Britain was the steam engine, which powered machinery in factories and later powered railways and ships too. I'm pretty sure there was also a shift away from home-based cottage industry, where people made goods in their own houses, toward centralized factories where production was concentrated. And I believe this caused really rapid urbanization, since workers moved into industrial cities looking for factory jobs, often ending up living and working in pretty harsh conditions. I'm not totally sure what else fed into this but those seem like the core pieces.

Q (q16, 4 pts): Explain the causes of European imperialism in Africa in the late 19th century.
A: European industrial economies wanted raw materials and new markets, which pushed them to look at Africa. There was also intense competition and rivalry among European powers for prestige, which got formalized at events like the Berlin Conference where they divided up territory. I think superior military technology, like the Maxim gun, gave Europeans a huge advantage over African societies trying to resist, which made conquest much easier than it otherwise would have been.

Q (q17, 3 pts): Describe the effects of the Opium Wars on China.
A: So the Opium Wars, thinking about it, started because Britain was selling opium into China to balance out its trade deficit, and China tried to crack down on this and suppress the opium trade because it was causing addiction and social problems. Britain didn't like that so they went to war, and China ended up getting militarily defeated, first by Britain and then later other powers got involved too when they saw China was weak. Because of these defeats, China was forced into signing unequal treaties, like the Treaty of Nanjing, which made China give up Hong Kong to Britain and open up several treaty ports where foreign powers had special control and privileges. So really it's this whole cycle of the opium trade, the suppression attempt, the military defeat, and then the unequal treaties that followed.

Q (q18, 3 pts): Explain the significance of the Meiji Restoration in Japan.
A: Tokugawa Shogunate ended. Imperial rule restored. Japan modernized fast. Military rebuilt. Industry rebuilt. Government institutions changed. All based on Western models. Japan became a major industrial power. Became a military power too. Avoided being colonized. Later projected its own imperial power abroad.

Q (q19, 4 pts): Explain the causes of World War I.
A: A complex system of alliances meant that a conflict between just two powers could quickly pull in the rest of Europe. Rising nationalism and competing imperial ambitions created ongoing tension among the great powers throughout the early 1900s. The immediate spark was the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand in Sarajevo in 1914. On top of that, a prewar arms race, including naval competition between Britain and Germany, had already put the major powers on edge and ready for conflict.

Q (q20, 4 pts): Explain the significance of the Bolshevik Revolution.
A: Lenin led Bolsheviks. They seized power in 1917. They overthrew the provisional government. That government had replaced the Tsar earlier that year. Russia pulled out of World War I. Began building a Communist state. First major Communist state in the world. Inspired movements globally. Also alarmed other governments worldwide.

Q (q21, 4 pts): Describe the causes and consequences of the Great Depression.
A: The Great Depression began with the U.S. stock market crash in 1929. Global trade and lending were deeply interconnected at the time, so the economic crisis spread quickly to other countries around the world. This led to mass unemployment and severe hardship for people across many nations. The resulting economic desperation contributed to the rise of authoritarian and fascist movements in several countries during the 1930s.

Q (q22, 4 pts): Explain the causes of World War II in Europe.
A: Okay so thinking through World War II in Europe, I keep coming back to how much resentment there was over the Treaty of Versailles, like Germans felt humiliated and burdened by it, and that fed into growing German nationalism. Then Hitler rises up with Nazi ideology, and he's pushing for territorial expansion, wanting to grow Germany's borders and reclaim power. Meanwhile the Western powers, Britain and France mainly, were following this policy of appeasement, trying to give in to some of Hitler's demands to avoid war, but that ended up failing to stop him from becoming more aggressive over time. Eventually it all comes to a head when Germany invades Poland in 1939, and that's what triggers Britain and France to declare war. So really it's this build up of resentment, then ideology and expansion, then failed appeasement, then the actual invasion that sets everything off.

Q (q23, 3 pts): Describe the process of decolonization in Africa after World War II.
A: I think one big factor was that European powers came out of World War II economically and militarily weakened, which made it a lot harder for them to hold onto their colonies the way they had before. At the same time, I believe nationalist movements were rising up across Africa, with local leaders organizing people to push for self-rule and independence. I'm not totally sure about the exact order or pace things happened in across the continent, but those two factors seem like the main drivers.

Q (q24, 2 pts): Explain the significance of the Non-Aligned Movement during the Cold War.
A: The Non-Aligned Movement was made up of countries that wanted to avoid formally aligning with either the U.S.-led bloc or the Soviet-led bloc during the Cold War. These states tried to chart an independent path instead of picking a side in the broader superpower rivalry.

Q (q25, 3 pts): Explain the causes of the Chinese Communist Revolution.
A: Chinese Communist Party led by Mao Zedong. Nationalist government led by Chiang Kai-shek. Two sides fought for control. Communists promised land reform. Peasants liked that a lot. Rural support grew for Mao's side.

Q (q26, 2 pts): Explain the significance of the Green Revolution in the 20th century.
A: Green Revolution meant new high-yield crop varieties got developed. Modern farming techniques spread with them. Fertilizers and better seeds used more widely. That's the core of it.

Q (q27, 4 pts): Describe the causes and effects of the fall of the Soviet Union.
A: I think the fall of the Soviet Union came from a mix of things, starting with long-term economic stagnation, since the centrally planned economy just wasn't producing efficiently anymore and had fallen behind. Then Gorbachev came in with his policies of glasnost, meaning openness, and perestroika, meaning restructuring, trying to reform the system, though I'm not totally sure how much these actually fixed things versus expose deeper problems. Around the same time, nationalist movements within various Soviet republics started pushing hard for independence, which put even more strain on the union. I believe all of this together led to the Soviet collapse, and its significance was that it ended the Cold War and the whole bipolar structure of global power that had defined international relations for decades.

Q (q28, 2 pts): Explain the significance of the Rwandan Genocide.
A: Rwandan Genocide happened in 1994. Ethnic conflict between Hutu majority and Tutsi minority. Mass killings occurred over a short period. International community largely failed to intervene. This was despite the scale of the killing being widely known.

Q (q29, 3 pts): Describe the effects of globalization on the world economy since 1990.
A: Trade barriers fell after 1990, allowing supply chains to become increasingly interconnected across countries around the world. Advances in digital communication sped up the flow of capital, information, and business across national borders. At the same time, increased cultural exchange provoked backlash in some places, leading to movements that sought to defend local or national identity against globalizing forces.

Q (q30, 2 pts): Explain the significance of the Arab Spring.
A: So the Arab Spring, when I think about it, was basically this wave of protests that broke out across a bunch of countries in the Middle East and North Africa, starting around 2010 or 2011, and people were rising up against authoritarian governments that had been in power for a really long time. It kind of spread from country to country, like Tunisia and Egypt and others, with people demanding more democracy and an end to oppressive rule. I'm blanking on exactly what made it spread so fast between countries, but the core thing is it was this pro-democracy uprising wave against authoritarian regimes.
