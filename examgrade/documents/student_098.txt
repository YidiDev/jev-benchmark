Q (q01, 3 pts): Describe the political and administrative structure the Mongol Empire used to govern its territory.
A: Okay so the Mongol Empire, which was founded by Genghis Khan, basically ran on this decimal system for organizing people, like units of 10, 100, 1,000, and 10,000, and honestly that's kind of the key thing to understand here. It sounds simple but it mattered a lot because it cut across all the old tribal and clan loyalties that used to divide the steppe peoples. So instead of loyalty going to your tribe or your family clan, you were loyal to your unit of ten or your unit of a thousand, whatever, and that unit was tied into the larger structure that Genghis Khan controlled. And because of this decimal structure, the Mongols could mobilize this huge multi-ethnic population really fast and command them effectively without having to rely on all these pre-existing tribal hierarchies that could easily rebel or splinter off. So really the decimal system, going back to it again, was both military and administrative, it let them organize conquered peoples too, not just Mongols themselves, into this same framework, which is what let such a relatively small population run such a giant empire.

Q (q02, 4 pts): Explain the significance of the Indian Ocean trade network before 1500 CE.
A: I think the Indian Ocean trade network was really significant mainly because of the sheer range and value of goods being moved around, like spices, textiles, porcelain, and gold moved between different regions. I'm not totally sure but I believe the main regions connected were East Africa, especially the Swahili coast, along with Arabia, India, Southeast Asia, and China, so it was this huge interconnected zone stretching across a big part of the eastern hemisphere. That reliability is probably what made the whole network function as smoothly as it did for so many centuries before 1500.

Q (q03, 3 pts): Describe the circumstances of the fall of the Byzantine Empire in 1453.
A: The fall of Constantinople in 1453 happened when the Ottoman Empire, led by Sultan Mehmed II, launched a massive siege against the city. The Ottomans used enormous siege cannons that were able to breach the city's ancient walls, which had protected Constantinople for centuries and had never been broken through like that before. This event marked the definitive end of the Roman imperial line, since the Byzantine Empire had considered itself the continuation of Rome. It also gave the Ottomans firm control over key trade routes in the Eastern Mediterranean, which cut off or complicated European access to Asian goods, and this pushed European states to start looking for alternative routes to Asia, eventually leading toward the Age of Exploration.

Q (q04, 3 pts): Explain the impact of the Black Death on European society and economy.
A: The Black Death had massive effects on Europe starting in the mid-1300s. It killed roughly a third of the population of Europe, which is an enormous demographic loss in a very short period. Because so many workers died, there was a serious labor shortage afterward, and this drove up wages significantly since laborers were now in higher demand. This weakened the manorial system and serfdom because lords needed workers and had to offer better terms, which sped up the overall decline of feudalism in Europe. The plague also caused a huge religious crisis, since prayer and religious rituals did nothing to stop the disease, and this led to scapegoating of minority groups like Jewish communities, as well as broader challenges to the authority and credibility of the Catholic Church.

Q (q05, 2 pts): Describe how the Aztec Empire extracted resources from the peoples it conquered.
A: I think the Aztec Empire mainly extracted resources through a tribute system, where conquered city-states had to regularly send goods, labor, or soldiers to the Aztecs as a form of submission. I'm not totally sure but I believe this meant the Aztecs didn't really need to directly administer every single conquered territory themselves, they just left local rulers in place as long as tribute kept flowing. This tribute, I think, was what funded the imperial capital of Tenochtitlan and supported the Aztec elite, letting the empire grow wealthy and powerful without the cost of running a huge bureaucracy over all their conquered lands.

Q (q06, 3 pts): Explain how the Ottoman Empire used the devshirme system.
A: I think the devshirme system worked by the Ottomans periodically taking Christian boys, mostly from the Balkan provinces, as a kind of levy on the population there. From what I remember, these boys were converted to Islam and then trained pretty intensively, and a lot of them ended up becoming elite soldiers known as Janissaries, or sometimes high-ranking administrators in the empire. I think the point of doing this was that it built up a military and administrative class that was personally loyal to the Sultan himself, rather than being tied to hereditary nobles or aristocratic families who might have their own competing interests. So it's kind of like the Ottomans were building their own loyal elite from scratch instead of relying on existing noble families.

Q (q07, 5 pts): Explain the significance of the Atlantic Slave Trade for the development of the Americas.
A: I think the Atlantic Slave Trade was hugely significant for the Americas in a few interconnected ways. There was this triangular trade system linking Europe, West Africa, and the Americas, and through it millions of enslaved Africans were forcibly transported across the Atlantic over the centuries. I believe the wealth generated from plantation exports in the Americas was enormous, and this really enriched European trading empires and fueled colonial economies quite a bit. At the same time, I think this trade had devastating effects back in Africa, depopulating and destabilizing parts of West and Central Africa over the long run as communities were raided and disrupted repeatedly. And I'm fairly confident it also led to lasting African cultural, religious, and linguistic influence throughout the Americas, since enslaved people brought their traditions with them and these blended into and shaped new American societies.

Q (q11, 3 pts): Explain the causes of the Scientific Revolution in Europe.
A: The Scientific Revolution was driven by a new emphasis on empirical observation and experimentation as the proper basis for gaining knowledge about the natural world. This approach directly challenged the older reliance on classical texts, especially the works of Aristotle, as well as religious doctrine, which had previously been treated as the sole authoritative sources of truth. Figures like Copernicus, Galileo, and Newton exemplified this shift, since their work, including the development of heliocentrism, relied on observation and mathematical proof rather than accepted ancient authority. This combination of new methods and challenges to old authorities is what really defined the Scientific Revolution.

Q (q12, 4 pts): Explain how Enlightenment political philosophy influenced later revolutions.
A: So Enlightenment political philosophy, thinking about thinkers like Locke and Rousseau and Montesquieu, these guys really shaped how people thought about government and rights, and it's kind of interesting how their ideas fed directly into actual revolutions later on. Like, going back to the American Revolution, the arguments colonists made for self-government and breaking away from Britain were directly informed by these Enlightenment thinkers, drawing on their ideas about government being based on consent rather than just top-down authority. And then, circling back to Europe, the French Revolution also drew directly on these same kinds of Enlightenment ideas, with revolutionaries calling for liberty, equality, and an end to absolute monarchy, which again ties right back to what Locke and Rousseau and Montesquieu had been writing about earlier. So really both of these major revolutions were shaped pretty directly by this same body of political philosophy circulating around Europe and the colonies at the time.

Q (q15, 4 pts): Explain the process of industrialization in Britain.
A: Industrialization in Britain relied heavily on the steam engine, which powered factory machinery and later powered railways and steamships as well. Production shifted away from home-based cottage industry toward centralized factories where work could be organized and supervised more efficiently. Britain also had abundant domestic coal and iron deposits, which supplied the raw materials and energy needed to sustain industrial growth. As factories multiplied, workers moved rapidly into industrial cities looking for jobs, leading to fast urbanization, though often under harsh and crowded living conditions.

Q (q19, 4 pts): Explain the causes of World War I.
A: World War I had several interlocking causes. A complex web of alliances meant that a conflict between just two powers could quickly pull in the rest of Europe, since allied nations were obligated to support each other. Long-standing nationalist and imperial ambitions among the great powers also created ongoing tension, as countries competed for territory, prestige, and influence. The immediate spark was the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand in Sarajevo in 1914, which set off the diplomatic crisis that led to war. Finally, a prewar arms race, including intense naval competition between Britain and Germany, had already put European powers on a heightened state of military readiness before the war even began.

Q (q22, 4 pts): Explain the causes of World War II in Europe.
A: Several factors led to World War II in Europe. Resentment over the harsh terms of the Treaty of Versailles fueled a strong wave of German nationalism after World War I. This resentment helped enable the rise of Hitler and Nazi ideology, which pursued aggressive territorial expansion across Europe. Western powers responded to early German aggression with a policy of appeasement, but this failed to stop Hitler's ambitions and only allowed Germany to grow stronger. Finally, Germany's invasion of Poland in 1939 triggered formal declarations of war from Britain and France, marking the direct start of the conflict.
