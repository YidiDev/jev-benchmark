Q (q01, 3 pts): Describe the political and administrative structure the Mongol Empire used to govern its territory.
A: I think the Mongols organized their people using some kind of decimal system? Like units of 10, 100, 1,000, and 10,000, and I believe this was mainly a military structure that also became administrative. I'm not totally sure but I think the point of this was to break up old tribal and clan loyalties so that people were reorganized under new leaders loyal to the wider state rather than their own tribe. This would have let them mobilize huge numbers of soldiers quickly and control a very diverse, multi-ethnic population without needing to rely on all the old tribal hierarchies that existed before, since everyone was folded into this new numeric structure instead.

Q (q02, 4 pts): Explain the significance of the Indian Ocean trade network before 1500 CE.
A: So the Indian Ocean trade network, before 1500, was basically this huge web connecting East Africa, like the Swahili coast, with Arabia, India, Southeast Asia, and China, all trading with each other constantly, going back and forth. And the stuff being traded was things like spices, textiles, porcelain, and gold, so really valuable goods moving across huge distances. What made this all possible, I keep coming back to this, was the monsoon winds, because they blew predictably in one direction part of the year and then reversed later, so sailors could actually plan a round trip instead of just going one way and being stuck, which made long distance sea trade way more reliable than overland routes. And because of all this contact between merchants and travelers, Islam spread along the East African coast and into Southeast Asia too, so it wasn't just goods moving, it was religion and culture spreading through these same networks. So overall the network mattered because of the goods, the wide geographic reach, the monsoon winds enabling it, and the religious spread that came out of it.

Q (q03, 3 pts): Describe the circumstances of the fall of the Byzantine Empire in 1453.
A: Ottomans took Constantinople. Mehmed II led them. Byzantine Empire ended. This was the end of the old Roman line. Ottomans then controlled key trade routes in the eastern Mediterranean. This pushed Europeans to look for new routes to Asia by sea.

A: Labor shortage after the plague. Wages went up. Serfdom weakened. Feudalism started declining faster. People also questioned the Church. Prayer didn't stop the disease. Minority groups got blamed and scapegoated. Church authority was challenged.

Q (q06, 3 pts): Explain how the Ottoman Empire used the devshirme system.

Q (q07, 5 pts): Explain the significance of the Atlantic Slave Trade for the development of the Americas.
A: Triangular trade linked Europe, West Africa, and the Americas. Millions of enslaved Africans were forced across the Atlantic. They worked on plantations. Grew cash crops. Sugar, tobacco, cotton. This made huge profits. Wealth went to European empires. Colonial economies grew rich off it. West and Central Africa lost population. Regions there got destabilized over centuries. African culture spread across the Americas. Religion too. Language too. Lasting influence everywhere in the New World.

Q (q08, 3 pts): Explain the causes of the Protestant Reformation.
A: Martin Luther posted the 95 Theses in 1517. He criticized the Church. He objected to indulgences being sold. He saw this as corruption. Many people agreed with him.

Q (q09, 4 pts): Describe the Columbian Exchange and its effects.
A: New crops came over. Maize and potatoes. These boosted food supply. Population grew in Afro-Eurasia because of it. Livestock came too. European crops and animals changed American ecosystems. Diets changed on both sides. Societies were reshaped by this.

Q (q10, 3 pts): Explain the significance of the Tokugawa Shogunate's sakoku policy.
A: Sakoku severely restricted foreign trade and travel. Japan closed itself off from most of the outside world under this policy. However, it wasn't a complete closure. The Dutch were still allowed limited trade. This happened through the controlled port of Nagasaki, specifically the island of Dejima.

Q (q11, 3 pts): Explain the causes of the Scientific Revolution in Europe.
A: I think one big cause was that people started challenging old ideas, like relying only on Aristotle's texts or church doctrine as the truth about the natural world. I'm not totally sure but I believe figures like Copernicus, Galileo, and later Newton played a huge role, especially with heliocentrism showing that the earth wasn't the center of the universe like people had assumed. This kind of discovery, I think, is what pushed people to question older authorities and think there might be other ways of understanding the world.

Q (q12, 4 pts): Explain how Enlightenment political philosophy influenced later revolutions.

Montesquieu pushed separation of powers. These ideas fed directly into the French Revolution. Calls for liberty and equality. Calls to end absolute monarchy. Direct link there.

Q (q13, 5 pts): Describe the causes and effects of the French Revolution.
A: Okay so the French Revolution, there's a lot going on here, but I think one big cause was just money, the government was massively in debt, partly from wars they'd been fighting, and that fiscal crisis kept getting worse and worse until something had to give. And then there's also the ideas side of it, like Enlightenment thinking about liberty and equality and the idea that power should come from the people, popular sovereignty, that stuff was floating around and giving people a way to frame their frustration. So really it's debt and war costs on one hand, and Enlightenment ideas about liberty and equality on the other hand, those two things together, I think, pushed things toward revolution. There's probably more to it but those are the two big things I keep coming back to.

Q (q15, 4 pts): Explain the process of industrialization in Britain.
A: I think the steam engine was a huge part of British industrialization, since it powered machinery in factories and later powered railways and ships too. Q (q16, 4 pts): Explain the causes of European imperialism in Africa in the late 19th century.
A: Okay so European imperialism in Africa, I think part of it was just economic, like they wanted raw materials for their factories and new markets to sell stuff to, since their economies were industrializing so fast. And then there was also this competition thing going on between the European powers, like they were racing each other for territory and prestige, and that's basically why they had the Berlin Conference, to divide things up and avoid fighting each other over it. And then there's the ideology part too, like Social Darwinist ideas and racist thinking that let them justify taking over other people's land by calling it a civilizing mission, like they were helping people when really they were just exploiting them. So it's kind of these three things, economic need, rivalry between powers, and this racist justifying ideology, all working together.

Q (q17, 3 pts): Describe the effects of the Opium Wars on China.
A: I think China lost militarily to Britain, and I believe other powers got involved too later on. Because of this defeat, I'm pretty sure China was forced into unequal treaties, like the Treaty of Nanjing, which made them give up Hong Kong and open up treaty ports where foreign powers had a lot of control. That seems like the main outcome, a weakened China forced to accept terms it didn't really agree to.

Q (q18, 3 pts): Explain the significance of the Meiji Restoration in Japan.
A: I think the Meiji Restoration was significant because it ended the Tokugawa Shogunate and restored imperial rule, at least formally, to the emperor. From there, Japan rapidly modernized, I believe across its military, its industry, and its government institutions, basically copying a lot of Western models to catch up quickly. Because of this rapid modernization, Japan became a major industrial and military power, which I think let it avoid being colonized like a lot of the rest of Asia, and it eventually even started projecting its own imperial power onto others. So overall it seems like a pretty huge turning point for Japan's whole trajectory.

Q (q19, 4 pts): Explain the causes of World War I.

Then there's the assassination, Archduke Franz Ferdinand got assassinated in Sarajevo in 1914, and that's usually treated as the spark that set things off. But I keep thinking there's also this arms race going on beforehand, like Britain and Germany competing over naval power, building up bigger and bigger navies, which made everyone more ready and willing to go to war when the moment came. So nationalism and rivalry, the assassination as the trigger, and the arms race making everyone primed for conflict, those are the big pieces I keep coming back to.

Q (q22, 4 pts): Explain the causes of World War II in Europe.
A: I think one major cause was the resentment many Germans felt over the Treaty of Versailles, since it placed heavy blame and burdens on Germany after WWI, and this fed a lot of nationalist anger. From there, I believe Hitler and the Nazi Party rose to power partly on this resentment, pursuing an aggressive ideology aimed at territorial expansion across Europe. I'm not totally sure but I think the Western powers responded with a policy of appeasement, trying to avoid conflict by giving in to some of Germany's demands, but this ended up failing to stop Hitler's aggression from escalating further. Finally, when Germany invaded Poland in 1939, that's what triggered Britain and France to declare war, officially starting the war in Europe.
