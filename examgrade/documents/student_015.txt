Q (q01, 3 pts): Describe the political and administrative structure the Mongol Empire used to govern its territory.
A: Okay so the Mongol Empire, this was founded by Genghis Khan, and the way they governed all this territory was pretty interesting because instead of just keeping the old tribal and clan systems that existed before, they broke everyone up into this decimal system, like units of 10, then 100, then 1,000, then 10,000. So basically you'd have a guy in charge of ten people, and then that feeds up into a guy in charge of a hundred, and so on up to ten thousand. And the point of this, or at least what I remember about the point of this, is that it cut across the old tribal loyalties, so people weren't organized by their old clan or tribe anymore, they were organized by this new military structure that Genghis Khan set up. This mattered a lot because it meant the Mongols could mobilize this huge multi-ethnic population really fast and command them without having to rely on all the old pre-existing tribal or clan hierarchies that might have competing loyalties. So basically the decimal system was both military and administrative at the same time, and it let this relatively small group of Mongols control and organize a massive and diverse empire efficiently.

Q (q04, 3 pts): Explain the impact of the Black Death on European society and economy.
A: So the Black Death really messed with European society in a bunch of ways, and one of the big ones was the labor thing, like because so many people died there just weren't enough workers left, so this created a labor shortage, and that shortage actually drove wages up because peasants and workers could now demand more since they were in such high demand. This whole thing ended up weakening the manorial system and serfdom, because lords couldn't just keep peasants tied down anymore when labor was scarce and workers had more leverage, so it kind of sped up the decline of feudalism overall. And then there was also this huge religious crisis that came out of it, because people prayed and prayed for the plague to stop and it just didn't, so this made people start questioning the Church's authority and whether the Church could actually protect them or explain what was happening. This also led to scapegoating, like blaming minority groups such as Jewish communities for causing the plague, which was obviously not true but people were desperate for an explanation. So overall it was this huge shift both economically with the labor shortage and wages, and religiously with people doubting the Church.

So it's this whole interconnected system of forced movement of people and goods that had huge impacts on all three continents involved.

Q (q09, 4 pts): Describe the Columbian Exchange and its effects.
A: The Columbian Exchange had a devastating effect on indigenous populations because Old World diseases like smallpox spread to the Americas, and since indigenous peoples had no prior immunity to these diseases, the death tolls were catastrophic. On the flip side, New World crops like maize and potatoes were brought back to Afro-Eurasia, and these crops boosted food supplies there, which led to population growth in places like Europe, Africa, and Asia. Additionally, European livestock and crops introduced to the Americas transformed the ecosystems there, changing diets and reshaping societies on both sides of the Atlantic as people adapted to these new plants and animals.

Q (q10, 3 pts): Explain the significance of the Tokugawa Shogunate's sakoku policy.
A: The sakoku policy under the Tokugawa Shogunate severely restricted foreign trade and travel, which largely closed Japan off from the outside world for an extended period. This policy was also meant to curb the spread of Christianity within Japan, since the Shogunate was worried about the religion's growing influence. Along with this, the Shogunate wanted to limit foreign political and economic influence more generally, keeping outside powers from gaining too much sway over Japanese affairs.

Q (q16, 4 pts): Explain the causes of European imperialism in Africa in the late 19th century.
A: European imperialism in Africa was driven by several interconnected causes. First, industrial economies in Europe needed raw materials and new markets, and Africa offered both, which made colonization economically appealing. Second, there was intense competition and prestige rivalry among European powers, who wanted to outdo one another in acquiring territory, and this rivalry was formalized through events like the Berlin Conference, where European nations divided up Africa among themselves. Third, ideologies like Social Darwinism and racial theories were used to justify this domination, framing colonization as a supposed civilizing mission that would benefit Africans, even though it was really about exploitation. Finally, superior military technology, like the Maxim gun, gave Europeans a decisive advantage over African societies, making conquest and control much easier to achieve and maintain.
